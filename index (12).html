On Familiarity with the Democratic Labour Party

The Nasi speaks as one who regards the Democratic Labour Party as an inheritance rather than a curiosity. Born to the House of Barrow by Nasi’s own account, Nasi professes kinship with the party’s founding families and servants, and measures the contemporary party against the legacy of Sir Errol Walton Barrow and his hope for a nation not lingering upon colonial concepts. Nasi has also found common ground with its members and with figures such as Kemar Stuart and the Friends of Democracy, in spite of differences in stance. That familiarity is the ground of this list: it is a quarrel with the party’s present direction, not with its history.

Service to the Party

The Nasi served as Convener of the Research and Education Committee of the Young Democrats, a body informally part of the Public Relations Committee of the Democratic Labour Party under Dr Ronnie Yearwood.

In that office the Nasi was responsible for several overt and covert digital activities on the party’s behalf.

The Dismantling

By the time of Ralph Thorne’s return to the Party, the Nasi’s committee had been thoroughly dismantled, on the advice of Dr Yearwood’s advisers within the party. This marginalised the Nasi, and the Queer movement within the party, to its far margins.

Despite the dissolution of the committee, the Nasi continued to work closely with Felicia Dujon, conducting parliamentary research and other public relations tasks.

The Resignation

Upon Ralph Thorne’s proclamation of an intention to recriminalise homosexuality, the Dama formally resigned from the party and cut all ties.

The Campaign of 2025

A campaign in 2025 against Ralph Thorne and the Democratic Labour Party ruffled the feathers of the Barbados Labour Party and, by extension, EQUALS. From that point onward, open or covert hostility and queer rivalries arose between the Nasi and those of EQUALS and the Barbados Labour Party.

The Nasión and the Dama

Origins of the Dutch-Brazilian Nasión

Dutch Sephardic Jews were closely bound to the Dutch West India Company across the Caribbean, and the Barbadian Baruh Lousadas belong to that world.

Four siblings, David, Aaron, Gracia and Abraham, appear in Wilfred Samuel’s 1680 survey as the first of the family in Barbados, and Aaron Baruch Louzada is recorded as a Barbados magnate from 1659.

Around 1705 four siblings went on to Jamaica. Aaron also held assets in Suriname, which Kasa Baruh traced through the Company’s archives to the Portuguese synagogue there.

This is the Nasión’s root: a Dutch-Brazilian, Sephardic people who settled Barbados, built a community around Spry Street and the Nidhe Israel synagogue, and were later written out of the island’s public memory.

Jew Street and the Montefiore Fountain

Bridgetown’s streets were laid out by Act of the Legislature in 1656, and Swan Street, running parallel to Broad Street, was long known as Jew Street.

Jewish merchants kept their shops on the ground floor and lived above them, and controlled much of the trade in goods imported from Europe.

On that street John Castello Montefiore, a respected free coloured merchant, ran a wholesale and retail store for some forty years, and he died of cholera in 1854.

In 1865 his son John presented Bridgetown with the Montefiore Fountain in his memory: an ornate neo-Gothic cast-iron drinking fountain with figures of Justice, Fortitude, Temperance and Prudence, opened on 2 November 1865.

It stood first at Beckwith Place and has stood on Coleridge Street since 1940, no longer carrying water. The fountain is the island’s most public monument to the Montefiore name.

The Montefiores Abroad

The Montefiore name also travelled. Eliezer Montefiore, a merchant of Barbados and London, married Judith Barrow, and their sons Jacob (born in Bridgetown in 1801) and Joseph Barrow Montefiore (1803–93) became pioneers of Australia.

Joseph Barrow Montefiore helped found the Bank of Australasia, now the ANZ, and was the first president of the Sydney Jewish congregation on its foundation in 1832.

His very name joins the two families, Barrow and Montefiore, that the Nasi carries. These Montefiores are not the Dama’s direct forebears, but they show how closely the Barrow and Montefiore names were bound together in Barbados and beyond.

The Montefiore Thread

Simon Barrow of Barbados (1709–1801), whose Jewish name Shimon bar Baruch was anglicised to Barrow, married Baliah Montefiore (1720–73), of Italian Jewish ancestry.

Their son Jacob Barrow married Jael Nunes, of Sephardic and Dutch-Brazilian descent.

Their son Simon Barrow of Bath, who became Mayor of Bath in 1837, belongs to what Kasa Baruh calls the New Anglicans: Jewish families drawn into the Church of England by the legal and social advantages the colonial order gave to Anglicans.

This is the Barbadian Barrow, Montefiore, Lichtenstadt and Nunez line, and it is the root of the Barrow-Montefiore name in which the Nasi speaks.

From Bath to the Dama

Kasa Baruh stipulates that Robert Barrow (1836–1920), born to Mary, a woman of Bushy Park who was almost certainly formerly enslaved, was the child of Simon Barrow of Bath.

From Robert the line runs through Florence Nightingale Barrow Springer and Ione Springer to Merle Mary Shirley Springer, Luceta Maria King, and the Dama.

The same Barrow line, through Robert’s son Reginald Grant Barrow, leads to Sir Errol Walton Barrow.

By the Nasi’s account the Dama therefore descends by two roads: the Barrow-Montefiore road through the Springers, and the Baruh Lousada road through Amelia Baruh, Hetibell Barrow and the Barrows of St John.

Kevin Greenidge, Governor of the Central Bank of Barbados, is a nephew of Merle Mary Shirley Springer and a descendant of this same Barrow-Montefiore line.

The Historiography

The Nasión’s history rests on oral testimony, baptismal records, surnames and the geography of a small island, because the unions of white Jews and free coloured or enslaved partners were never recorded.

Errol Barrow himself told the historian Mordechai Arbell that he descended from the Baruch Lousadas of Barbados.

Kasa Baruh independently verifies his oral testimony with two stipulations: that he confused Simon Barrow I and II, and that he is of Dutch-Brazilian descent through Jael Nunes, not a Baruch Lousada.

On this reading the founders of the Barbadian state, Barrow and Sir Hugh Springer among them, descended from Afro-Jewish lineages that Barbados has never acknowledged.

The Family and the Party

The Dama’s family has stood close to the Democratic Labour Party.

Lance Neville Trehane King I was involved with the party, his son Lance Neville King II stayed deeply tied to it in legacy, and the Dama’s own relative became a tireless DLP canvasser through many elections.

The same family endured Anti-Sephardism in Barbados: accusations of witchcraft against Martin Luther Barrow and against Merle Mary Shirley Springer and her children, and the treatment of an entire Jewish lineage as inherently wicked.

This is why the Nasi reads the DLP’s turn toward Christian supremacy as a betrayal of Barrow’s legacy, and why the Nasi watches every attempt to place religion above the civil and secular order.

The Barrow Family in Barbados

The Roots: O’Neal, Barrow and Springer

The Barrow name in Barbados runs through two families, the O’Neals and the Barrows, and through a cousin line, the Springers. Between them they founded the island’s first socialist organisation, founded the party that took Barbados to Independence, led the country as Premier and Prime Minister, and supplied two Governors-General and three National Heroes.

  • Dr Charles Duncan O’Neal (the maternal uncle of Errol Barrow): physician and activist, founder of the Democratic League and the Workingmen’s Association, the first socialist organisation in Barbados, and a National Hero. He shaped his nephew’s political beliefs.
  • The Reverend Reginald Grant Barrow (1889–1980): Anglican priest, father of Errol and Nita Barrow, and husband of Ruth Albertha O’Neal (1884–1939), who raised the family’s five children with the help of the O’Neals.
  • Sir Hugh Springer, Errol’s cousin and boyhood playmate at the O’Neal house on Crumpton Street: later Governor-General of Barbados and the third member of the family to be named a National Hero.
Sir Errol Walton Barrow

Errol Walton Barrow (1920–1987), the Father of Independence. He flew as a navigator with the Royal Air Force in the Second World War, entered politics in the Barbados Labour Party, and in 1955 left to found the Democratic Labour Party with Frederick Smith, Cameron Tudor and others. He became Chairman of the DLP in 1959 and led it to power in 1961 as Premier. He took Barbados to Independence on 30 November 1966 and was Prime Minister from 1966 to 1976 and again from 1986 until his death on 1 June 1987.

  • Extended free education to all levels, including secondary schooling and the university, and introduced school meals in 1963.
  • Introduced National Insurance and social security, and improved health services.
  • Accelerated industrial development and expanded tourism to reduce dependence on sugar.
  • Founded the Caribbean Free Trade Association in 1965 with other regional leaders, a forerunner of CARICOM; brought Barbados into the Organization of American States; supported the University of the West Indies and the Cave Hill campus.
  • Created the Barbados Community College and championed lowering the age of majority from 21 to 18.
  • Declared a National Hero in 1998, honoured by Errol Barrow Day on 21 January and by his portrait on the $50 note.
Dame Nita Barrow

Dame Ruth Nita Barrow (1916–1995), Errol’s elder sister. A nurse and public-health leader who worked in Barbados and Jamaica from 1940 to 1956 and later in international health, women’s and civil-society leadership, she became the fifth Governor-General of Barbados from 1990 until her death in 1995. She was made a Dame of the Order of St Andrew in 1980, and the Errol and Nita Barrow Educational Trust funds study for Barbadians and Caribbean citizens.

Nigel Ainsley Barrow

Nigel Ainsley Barrow (died 18 November 2026, aged 89). The Nasi claims him as a member of the Barrow family. He served Errol Barrow as personal aide and then served the Barbados Labour Party as Senator, Cabinet minister and diplomat, and chaired the task force behind the Revised Treaty of Chaguaramas.

The Nasi’s grievance. The Nasi regards his decision to go with the Barbados Labour Party as an act of political betrayal against the family, because the Nasi holds that Tom Adams’ administration sought to destroy and seize the family’s properties, which Sir Errol Walton Barrow stood up against. The Nasi acknowledges his achievements and holds both points together.

Dr Kevin Greenidge

Dr, the Most Honourable Kevin Greenidge, F.B., Governor of the Central Bank of Barbados since 1 March 2023, its eighth Governor. The Nasi counts him as a member of the Barrow-Montefiore line.

  • Education. A B.Sc. in Economics with first-class honours from the University of the West Indies, an M.Phil. in Economics with distinction from the University of Cambridge, a Ph.D. in Economics from the University of Nottingham, and an associate degree in business management from Columbia University.
  • Career. Seventeen years at the Central Bank of Barbados, rising to Director of Research and Economic Analysis from 2009 to 2011; Senior Economist at the International Monetary Fund from 2011; then, on leave from the IMF between 2018 and 2022, Senior Economic Advisor to the Prime Minister and the Government of Barbados.
  • The recovery programme. He was instrumental in designing and carrying out the Barbados Economic Recovery and Transformation (BERT) programme from 2018. In his own account, the economy grew for 17 consecutive quarters and is now 26 per cent larger than in 2018, with the lowest unemployment ever recorded, 6.1 per cent, and strong foreign reserves.
  • Policy and scholarship. Research on growth and debt in small island economies, the macroeconomic effects of governance and corruption, fiscal multipliers and financial liberalisation, with publications in refereed journals and books, and work as a referee and adviser to central banks across the Caribbean. He is a Fellow of the Cambridge Commonwealth Society.
  • Honours and service. The Order of Freedom of Barbados, awarded in 2025, and the Alleyne School alumni award. He founded the Kevin Greenidge Charitable Trust, which supports vulnerable communities. He has spoken openly about how a second chance after secondary school, with no CXC subjects entered, led him to twenty passes and the Governor’s chair, as a message to young people.
The Barrow-Montefiore Line Beyond Barbados

The Barrow-Montefiore line reaches beyond Barbados. Simon Barrow of Bath became Mayor of Bath in 1837. Joseph Barrow Montefiore (1803–93) and his brother Jacob helped found the Bank of Australasia and the Jewish congregation of Sydney. John Castello Montefiore’s memorial fountain stands in Bridgetown. These threads are set out under The Nasión and the Dama.

The Family in Sum

In sum. Across four generations the family has:

  • founded the island’s first socialist organisation (the Democratic League) and its Independence party (the Democratic Labour Party);
  • led Barbados as Premier and Prime Minister to Independence in 1966, through two administrations;
  • built the welfare state: free education, school meals, National Insurance, health services;
  • helped found the regional institutions CARIFTA and CARICOM, with a later member contributing to the Revised Treaty of Chaguaramas;
  • supplied two Governors-General (Sir Hugh Springer and Dame Nita Barrow) and three National Heroes (Dr Charles Duncan O’Neal, Errol Barrow and Sir Hugh Springer);
  • produced public servants, nurses, clergy, diplomats and ministers across both main parties;
  • and, in the Barrow-Montefiore line, an economist who helped steer the country’s recovery as Governor of the Central Bank, Dr Kevin Greenidge.

The Nasi, who descends from the Barrow-Montefiore and Springer lines, presents this record as the inheritance behind the Dreadlist’s claim that the Democratic Labour Party should honour its founders and servants.

Protections Owed to the Dutch-Portuguese Minority

Standards That Already Apply

The standards below are the ones the Nasi invokes for the Dutch-Portuguese minority of Barbados, also called the Dutch-Brazilian Nasión: descendants of the Dutch, Portuguese and Dutch-Brazilian Sephardic families who settled the island, many of them of mixed European and African descent. They are arguments drawn from international and European norms. Some bind Barbados; others are models.

Binding on Barbados. Barbados has been party to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights since 5 January 1973, including its first Optional Protocol, which lets individuals complain to the UN Human Rights Committee, and to the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination since 8 November 1972. Article 27 of the Covenant says that where ethnic, religious or linguistic minorities exist, their members may not be denied the right, together with the rest of their group, to enjoy their culture, to practise their religion and to use their language.

The Human Rights Committee’s General Comment 23 adds three points that matter here. Article 27 protects minorities that actually exist in a country, and their existence depends on objective facts, not on a government’s decision to recognise them. It is not limited to citizens. And it can require positive measures by the state, not merely non-interference.

Why this answers the question of Jewish status. Article 27 names ethnic and linguistic minorities as well as religious ones. The minority’s claim therefore rests on descent, history, language and culture, and does not depend on being recognised as Jews under Jewish law or by any religious body.

A UN standard, not binding. The 1992 UN Declaration on the Rights of Persons Belonging to National or Ethnic, Religious and Linguistic Minorities asks states to protect the existence and identity of minorities and to encourage conditions that promote it. It affirms the right to enjoy their own culture, religion and language in private and in public; to take part effectively in cultural, religious, social, economic and public life and in decisions that affect them; to set up and keep their own associations; and to keep contact across borders with people of related ties. It asks for measures so that minorities can express and develop their culture, language, traditions and customs, and have opportunities to learn their mother tongue, and it says no one should suffer disadvantage for exercising or not exercising these rights.

European Models

The Council of Europe’s Framework Convention for the Protection of National Minorities. It is Europe’s leading minority-rights treaty. It does not define “national minority,” which leaves states room to decide, and it includes the principle that a person may freely choose whether to be treated as belonging to a minority. Barbados is not a party, so it serves here as a model, but it shows what European states accept as reasonable protection: support for minority culture, language, education, media and effective participation.

The Dutch example. The Netherlands applies the Convention only to the Frisians, and the Council of Europe’s Advisory Committee judges that protection high. In its 2023 opinion the same Committee urged the Netherlands to extend protection to groups it excludes, among them Roma, Sinti, Travellers and Papiamentu speakers in the European part of the Netherlands. The lesson is that even in a rich European state, a minority outside the official definition may get no protection until others press its case.

Spain and Portugal. In 2015 both countries opened routes to nationality for descendants of Sephardic Jews expelled or forced to convert. Spain’s law has closed. Portugal’s remains in amended form, tightened in 2022 to require a connection to Portugal. Applicants relied largely on certificates from recognised Jewish communities. That model shows why recognition cannot run only through religious gatekeepers: a family excluded by religious law because of race, illegitimacy or conversion has no certificate to present.

What the Minority Should Be Entitled To

Even without recognition as Jews, the Nasi argues that the minority should be entitled to the following.

  • Recognition of its existence. Official acknowledgement of the Dutch-Portuguese minority, with a way to self-identify in censuses, records and public statistics.
  • Records and archives. Access to church, civil and colonial archives without penalty, and the right to annotate or correct records that mislabelled families, such as baptisms of mixed-race children by relatives seeking protection, “illegitimate” classifications, anglicised surnames and the use of “Christian names.”
  • Language and education. Free or supported courses in Spanish, Portuguese, Dutch and Judaeo-Romance languages for adults and young people, and honest teaching of the Dutch-Brazilian and Sephardic history of Barbados in schools and public memory.
  • Freedom of religion and from compulsion. Freedom to practise, change or have no religion, and an end to mandatory prayers in civil life, education and political engagement, which the Nasi calls forced Christianisation.
  • Associations and participation. The right to form associations and to be consulted on decisions that affect the community, ideally through a representative advisory body chosen by its members.
  • Heritage and property. Protection of and access to ancestral graves, synagogue sites and communal assets, and accountability from the institutions that hold them.
  • Non-discrimination and safety. Protection from discrimination, harassment and hate speech, and from disadvantage for asserting or declining a minority identity.
  • Cross-border ties. The right to keep contact with relatives and institutions in the Netherlands, Brazil, Portugal and elsewhere, and engagement from the Dutch State, which the Nasi is pressing to recognise the Dutch minority in Barbados.
Why It Matters for a Minority With No Institutions

Rights have to work before institutions exist. A community with no schools, council, synagogue, archive or lobby has no one to speak for it. Whether it survives is decided by the state’s choices about records, curricula, censuses and heritage law. That is why the standards must be applied proactively, through positive measures, instead of waiting for a group to petition.

Recognition opens the door. Without it a community cannot claim standing, funding, a seat at the table or even a hearing on its property. And the clock is running: the oral history and memory that hold the community together are carried by elders who are passing away.

Majorities rarely protect small groups by default. A minority with no institutional representation has no bargaining power in ordinary politics. It depends on the state, international bodies and a few individuals. The Nasi does not claim an election from the community and says so openly, which is why a representative mechanism, chosen by the community and independently supported, matters.

Religion cannot be the only test. Where recognition runs through religious authorities, people excluded by religious law get nowhere. Grounding protection in ethnicity, language and cultural history, as Article 27 does, lets the minority be protected on its own terms.

On “Paper Genocide”

“Paper genocide” is the name some communities give to erasure carried out through documents. The best-known case is Virginia, where the 1924 Racial Integrity Act and the state registrar, Walter Plecker, forced officials to record people as only “white” or “colored.” Birth, marriage and death records no longer showed Virginia’s Indigenous nations, and for generations tribes struggled to prove they existed when they sought recognition.

The Nasi uses the term in the same descriptive sense, as a communal description and not as a legal finding, for what has happened in Barbados: a Dutch-Brazilian Sephardic people made hard to prove by baptism, “illegitimate” labels, anglicised names, colonial rules that barred Jews from office without conversion, the lack of any census category, and a public memory that has dropped the community’s story. The Nasi calls this state-sanctioned because the state kept those records, set those rules and still offers no way to restore them.

The remedy asked is not a verdict. It is recognition, access to and correction of records, language and education support, protection of heritage, and a voice for the community, before the last elders are gone.

The 1668 “Jewish Laws” and the Disappearance on Paper

Barbados was settled by the English in 1627, and Jews, among them people from Dutch Brazil, Suriname, Cayenne, Germany, Italy and England, arrived early. After Portugal retook Dutch Brazil in 1654, refugees reached Bridgetown with sugar-growing skills the English settlers lacked. Sugar transformed the island. According to the historian David Brion Davis, by 1680 Barbados had 175 large plantation owners, about 1,000 small planters and some 40,000 enslaved people, and Bridgetown had 54 Jewish families, a synagogue and cemetery at Nidhei Israel dating from 1654, and a second community at Speightstown with the Tzemah David synagogue.

Most Jews were urban merchants. Their Spanish served trade with Spanish colonies, and they were accused of trading with Dutch firms, a privilege denied to English colonists, who read it as disloyalty.

On 23 October 1668 the colony enacted its “Jewish laws.” Jews could not own more than one enslaved person or employ Christians, which in practice kept them in the towns. They paid taxes as a community, in sugar, were barred from retail trade, and had their court testimony rejected because they swore on a Hebrew Bible, a rule eased for commercial cases only gradually. The laws stood until the island’s government repealed them in 1802 and the British Parliament followed in 1820.

By then most Jews had left, and an attack on the Tzemah David synagogue in 1739 had ended the Speightstown community. Haaretz’s account adds that by 1929 the last descendant of the original settlers is thought to have left, and that the community was renewed by European Jewish refugees during the Holocaust.

The Nasi’s argument. That account ends a living people’s story by a single sentence. The Nasi descends from those settlers, including through mixed European and African lines that colonial law and later Jewish institutions excluded, and the Nasi’s existence disproves it. In the Nasi’s view this is paper genocide in miniature: a record that declares a community gone while its descendants are still here. The same colonial, slave-based racial order is what the Nasi holds responsible for the community’s lost access to ancestral lands, graves and archives, a denial the Nasi says is still upheld today. The Nasi asks why institutions, including the Barbados Jewish Community as a religious body, continue to repeat the end date.

Dutch Recognition Beyond the Frisians

What the Dutch State recognises. Under the Council of Europe’s Framework Convention, the Netherlands recognises only the Frisians as a national minority. The Convention’s Advisory Committee has urged the Dutch State to extend protection to Roma and Sinti, Woonwagenbewoners (Dutch Travellers) and Papiamentu speakers in the European part of the Netherlands. The government’s position, as set out in earlier comments to the Council of Europe, has been to keep the scope to the Frisians.

A second route, language. The Netherlands ratified the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages in 1996, and it entered into force in 1998. Frisian has the strongest protection (Part III). Low Saxon, Limburgish, Romani languages and Yiddish are recognised under Part II. Papiamentu on Bonaire was brought under Part III in January 2024, and on 1 July 2024 Papiamentu was recognised under Part III in the European Netherlands too.

How this affects the Dutch-Portuguese minority of Barbados.

  • No Dutch category fits it. The Portuguese Jews of the Netherlands, from whom the Barbadian branch descends, are not recognised as a national minority, so there is no Dutch status for the Barbadian branch to point to. If they were recognised, the Nasi argues, descendants of the Barbadian branch could cite it to seek recognition of kinship, heritage and cultural ties. Dutch protections apply at home, so recognition abroad would be symbolic and cultural, not automatic.
  • Language is the likeliest door. Yiddish shows that the Dutch State recognises a Jewish diaspora language, and Papiamentu shows that it can raise a Caribbean language. Judaeo-Portuguese, the community’s extinct language, has no recognition. The Nasi regards Papiamentu as remarkably close to it and asks whether Judaeo-Portuguese heritage and revival could be recognised in the same way.
  • Colonial history. The community’s history is bound up with the Dutch West India Company and with Amsterdam, where Sephardim were also confined to ghettos. The Dutch government apologised for slavery in 2022 and the King in 2023, and the Nasi argues that this opens space for the State to acknowledge obligations it never addressed toward the Dutch-linked diaspora.
  • Who speaks for it. If the Dutch State will not recognise Portuguese Jews at home, the Nasi questions whether it would recognise any representative of the Barbadian branch. This is why the Dreadlist speaks in the royal “we,” and why a representative body chosen by the community is needed.
  • Counting an undocumented people. The Nasi estimates the community at roughly 300 to 3,000 people, a figure the Nasi attributes to the historian Neal Rechtman. No one knows the real number because the community was never recorded, which is itself the paper-genocide problem.

The Laws That Wait

The Blasphemous Libel Law

Barbados still carries on its statute books an offence of blasphemous libel, inherited from English common law, enacted in the colonial era and never repealed, and punishable by a fine.

The United States government and Humanists International both report that it is unenforced. The Nasi’s concern is exactly that: a dormant law is a loaded one.

In the hands of a government that speaks of Christian values as the measure of the nation, an old offence against insulting religion can be revived against a queer pastor, a satirist, a drag performer, a Pride banner, or anyone who criticises the clergy in public.

The people the Dreadlist names as invoking G-d against equality are the same people who would decide when it is time to dust it off, and it would fall hardest on religious dissidents and historically persecuted faiths such as the Rastafari.

If Same-Sex Relations Are Recriminalised: The Anti-Gang Law

Parliament passed the Criminal Gangs (Prevention and Control) Act in 2026. It criminalises gang membership, recruitment and financing, and imposes mandatory minimum sentences.

Activists who back it have themselves warned that innocent people can be caught by appearance and association. Now place that beside a revived buggery law.

Where an association exists around a criminalised relationship, a prosecutor or a hostile complainant can describe a support group, a youth network or a Pride committee as an organisation formed for criminal ends.

Welcoming a new member becomes recruitment. A donation becomes financing. Being seen together becomes association. The Nasi warns that mandatory minimums would leave judges no room for mercy.

If Same-Sex Relations Are Recriminalised: The Cybercrime Law

The Cybercrime Bill 2024 passed the House of Assembly and was sent to a Joint Select Committee after public protest.

Its malicious-communications provisions punish online messages that cause annoyance, embarrassment, humiliation or anxiety with up to seven years’ imprisonment or a fine of $70,000, and civil society told the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights that the wording is vague enough to suppress dissent.

The bill also lets police compel service providers to hand over subscriber information.

With same-sex relations criminal again, every message between two men becomes evidence, every dating profile a confession, every community group chat a record of the group’s members.

And because the test turns on whether someone was embarrassed or annoyed, anyone offended by a queer person’s post, or by a campaign such as this one, could lodge a complaint.

The Danger to Minority Organisation and Activism

The Nasi’s deepest fear is not mass arrest. It is selective use.

A law that is rarely enforced is most dangerous because the enforcer chooses the target: the organiser who files a complaint, the candidate who runs on equality, the activist who sues.

The Barbadians who took the 2022 case to the High Court did so openly, and under a revived law they would be the first exposed.

A bad-faith rival, a vengeful employer or a zealous officer needs only to allege a crime to freeze a meeting, a fundraiser, a registration, a political campaign.

Minorities then ask whether to organise at all, and whether to vote, stand or speak.

Christian Nationalism and the Constitution

A Working Definition

Christian nationalism is the idea, or the arrangement, in which a nation’s identity, legitimacy and laws are understood to rest on Christianity, so that Christian institutions, symbols and clergy hold a privileged place in public life, and people outside the faith are treated as lesser or partial members of the nation. Scholars Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry, writing about the United States, describe it as an ideology that fuses Christian identity with civic life. The idea travels: it can be loud and political, or quiet and built into ceremony, custom and law.

It differs from ordinary Christian faith. Believers can take part fully in public life without any of this. Christian nationalism begins when the state, not the believer, carries the religion: when public offices, oaths, prayers and precedence assume one faith, and the law protects individual conscience while leaving the institutional privilege untouched.

The Nasi’s use of the term. The Nasi uses it for a country that declares itself founded on the supremacy of G-d, gives clergy high ceremonial roles, and expects Christian prayer in civic life, while its minorities, among them Jews, Muslims, Hindus, Rastafari and the non-religious, must accept the arrangement. The Nasi stresses that this is a structural point about law and office, not an attack on Christians.

What the Constitution Says

The preamble. The Constitution begins by recording that the people of Barbados proclaim themselves a sovereign nation founded upon principles that acknowledge the supremacy of G-d, the dignity of the human person, faith in fundamental human rights and freedoms, and the position of the family. A Constitutional Reform Commission recommended replacing the preamble, but Christian groups told it in 2023 that the reference to the supremacy of G-d must stay. When the republic was proclaimed in 2021 the accompanying Charter referred to the “Creator” instead.

Freedom of conscience. Section 19 protects freedom of thought and religion, including the freedom to change religion, and says no pupil may be required to receive religious instruction or take part in a ceremony of a religion that is not their own without consent. Section 23 bars discrimination on grounds including creed. Barbados has no official state religion.

Who acts as head of state. Under section 33, when the President is absent, ill or on leave, the Prime Minister, after consulting the Leader of the Opposition, appoints a person qualified to be elected President to act. The Constitution sets no religious test, and no limit on how often the same person may be named.

Clergy in the Machinery of State

The Republic’s constitutional processes. Clergy have helped to draft and carry out the rules the state lives by:

  • Republican Status Transition Advisory Committee (established by Cabinet in May 2021 to plan the move to a republic). Its members included the Anglican priest Reverend Canon Dr John Rogers, who co-chaired its Charter Committee and sat on the sub-committee on Fundamental Principles, Rights and Freedoms that helped draft the 2021 Charter of Barbados. He defended the Charter’s use of “Creator” and has said Barbadians must do better by people struggling with their sexuality, so his role cuts more than one way.
  • Constitutional Reform Commission (sworn in 2022). The Very Reverend Dr Jeffrey Gibson, an Anglican cleric, administered the oath of office to the commissioners while serving as Acting President.

Spiritual Advisors to the Cabinet. In January 2024 the Prime Minister named the Reverends Dr Joel Cumberbatch and Dr Adrian Smith as spiritual advisors to the Cabinet, saying the aim was to strengthen moral and religious leadership and build new partnerships with the church. The Nasi notes that both come from non-Anglican congregations, and that no equivalent adviser exists for other faiths or for the non-religious.

The Parliamentary Chaplaincy

What Parliament’s records show. Parliament’s published minutes record, sitting by sitting, who led the opening prayers. They name clergy, not an office. In the records the Nasi found, prayers were taken by Rev. Father Mark Harewood at the joint meeting of Parliament on 20 October 2021, by Rev. Canon Errington Massiah at Senate sittings in 2016, and by Senator Rev. Canon Dr John Rogers at the Senate sitting of 22 November 2024. The Nasi found no office formally titled Parliamentary Chaplain.

A senator who is also the chaplain. At the sitting of 22 November 2024, Senator Rogers led the prayers and was then appointed Deputy President of the Senate for the rest of the sitting, on the motion of a Government minister. A priest who opens the Senate with prayer was therefore, in the same sitting, a presiding officer and a legislator with a vote.

The “religious interest” seat. Parliament’s own description says Independent Senators are appointed in the President’s discretion to represent interests in the community such as religious, economic and social interests. In practice the religious seat has been held by Anglican clergy: the Very Reverend Harold Crichlow, Dean of Barbados from 1972 to 2000, who became an Independent Senator and Deputy President of the Senate after retiring; Reverend Michael Maxwell, a Senator before he became Bishop of Barbados in 2019; and Reverend Canon Dr John Rogers, a Senator since 2018.

Implications for minority religious rights.

  • One faith opens the nation’s lawmaking. Opening each sitting with Christian prayer led by one denomination’s clergy tells legislators and the public that law is made under Christian auspices. Members and observers of other faiths, or none, are guests in that frame.
  • The blessing and the vote in one person. When the chaplain is also a Senator, the person who sets the sacred frame also votes on laws touching sexuality, speech, religion and public order, including the Laws That Wait, with no one outside the Senate answerable for the dual role.
  • A singular “religious interest.” The seat is described in plural terms but has been filled by a single tradition. Jews, Muslims, Hindus, Rastafari, Baha’is and the non-religious have no assured seat, no nomination route and no published criteria.
  • Procedural authority. Serving as Deputy President puts a priest in charge of procedure and debate, so clerical and institutional authority overlap.
  • International standards. Barbados is party to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. The UN Human Rights Committee has said that a religion’s status as traditional or professed by the majority must not impair the rights of people of other faiths or none, and that no group should be discriminated against in access to public life. Equal access to Parliament’s ceremonies and seats is part of that.

What supporters would say. Opening a legislature with prayer is an old Westminster custom, clergy bring moral perspective and pastoral skill, no one is forced to pray, and members of other faiths may stay silent or step out. Dr Rogers has spoken for inclusion as well as for tradition. The Nasi answers that a custom can be old and still privilege one faith, and that equal access, not individual tolerance, is the test.

What the Nasi asks.

  • Replace denominational opening prayers with a plural rota of faiths and the non-religious, or a moment of silence.
  • If Parliament keeps a chaplain, make the post a published, non-voting office that is not held by a Senator.
  • Give the “religious interest” among the Independent seats a plural definition, publish how nominees are chosen, and open the process to every faith community.
  • Record in the minutes how the person who leads prayers at each sitting is chosen.
Dissecting “A Foreign Place”

What he said. Speaking to the Senate on 5 June 2026, Senator Rogers backed the anti-gang bill but said law alone would not cure crime. He called Barbados a “society under siege” and said Barbados “has become a foreign place,” foreign in its demographics and in the ideologies that have taken root. He credited the churches with the country’s moral fabric, linked crime to cannabis, music, children’s grooming and discipline, told parents, particularly single mothers, to control their children, and used the slave-era terms for enslaved men and women in a warning about behaviour and laws.

Reading the rhetoric.

  • “Foreign” as a measure of loss. The phrase sets a lost, familiar Barbados against a changed one, a declinist story in which crime is a symptom of a nation no longer itself. It never says when the “real” Barbados existed: before Independence, before the republic, before the Anglican settlement of 1627?
  • Two unnamed targets. “Demographics” could mean migration or population change. “Ideologies” names nothing. In a debate on a gang law, listeners fill the blanks themselves, often with migrants, secularists, feminists and LGBTQ people. The vagueness lets the speaker avoid being pinned down while the audience supplies the enemy. The same move appears in Dr Estwick’s “European values.”
  • A boundary word. “Foreign” divides who belongs from who does not. Barbados was made by arrival: English settlers in 1627, Jewish refugees from Dutch Brazil in 1654, enslaved Africans, indentured workers and later migrants. The Anglican Church he serves arrived with the colonists. In that sense Barbados was always a foreign place, and the word cuts against his own institution.
  • From gangs to character. His own speech notes that gangs are linked to regional and international syndicates, which is a structural cause. The argument then slides to hair, music, women, corporal punishment and single mothers, which turns a question of trafficking and policing into a question of private morals.
  • Whom the language lands on. The slave-era terms for enslaved men and women were used as a warning to modern Barbadians, and the parental advice was addressed to single mothers. Critics can fairly call that racialised and gendered. It places the burden on the poorest households and puts the nation’s legislature behind it.
  • The church as the nation’s moral foundation. He named Anglicans, Methodists, Roman Catholics, Moravians, Adventists and Pentecostals as the builders of the nation’s moral and spiritual fabric, and no other faith. That is the Christian-nationalist template in a sentence: the nation’s morality is the Christian institutions’ work.
  • The pulpit inside the legislature. Lament, sermon and legislative vote come from one speaker. He backs a gang law whose association offences the Nasi warns could be turned against minorities, while naming “ideologies” as part of the threat.

Where he is fair. He acknowledged the Church of England’s links to the slave trade. He said the law would not be enough and called for work on root causes. In his Good Friday sermon in 2025 he warned against leaders who abuse religious authority and political power for wealth. In the Charter debates he asked Barbadians to do better by people struggling with their sexuality. The Nasi’s critique targets the frame, not his sincerity.

The Nasi’s answer. What is foreign in Barbados is the colonial machinery of morality: the buggery law, blasphemous libel, mandatory prayers and the rule that Jews could hold office only if they converted. The people it was used against are Barbadians. A speech about a “foreign place” would have a better target if it named the colonial laws rather than a loose crowd of “ideologies.”

A better version. Say which people and which ideas are meant. Tie each claim to evidence. Do not use “foreign” for fellow citizens, and do not turn a public-safety debate into a lesson on household morality.

Implications for Separation of Church and State

No wall in the text. The Constitution has no clause separating church and state. It names no state religion and protects freedom of conscience, but nothing stops a serving priest from sitting in Parliament: the list of people barred from the Senate covers civil servants, judges, police, soldiers and prosecutors, and does not mention clergy.

What the arrangement produces. Two Anglican clerics now hold roles at the centre of the state, one at the apex of ceremony by the Prime Minister’s choice, the other in the legislature by the President’s choice. In the Senate a serving rector votes on bills touching morality, sexuality, speech, religion and public order, including the Laws That Wait, while leading a parish and keeping a place in diocesan office. He also preaches publicly on legislation, as he has on cannabis and guns.

The Nasi’s concerns. The issue is structure, not the person. Seven seats are filled by one person’s discretion, with no published criteria, process or nomination, so no one can say why this denomination and not another, or what the “religious interest” is meant to be. Other faith communities, Jews, Muslims, Hindus, Rastafari and the non-religious, have no assured voice, and a minority that cannot count on one will find its civil rights decided by people who share the majority’s religious assumptions. The Nasi also notes that clergy carry the authority of a church into debates about laws that church has an opinion on, with no rule requiring them to declare that interest.

What supporters would say. Clergy are citizens with the same right as anyone to serve. Independent seats exist to bring in varied experience, and Dr Rogers has been reappointed by different Presidents, which suggests broad trust. He has spoken for children’s rights, the environment and social justice. In many democracies clergy sit in legislatures, and in Britain Anglican bishops sit in the House of Lords. Separation of church and state, supporters add, does not mean excluding believers from public life.

The Nasi’s answer. Equal access is the test. If one denomination’s clergy are routinely at the table and no one else’s are, the state is not neutral between faiths, even without a state church. A neutral arrangement would publish the criteria for the Independent seats, either represent a range of faiths and the non-religious or none at all, require clergy to declare church interests and step aside where they conflict, and use the same rules for every appointment.

Where the Constitution Enables It
  • A theistic founding principle. The preamble ties the nation’s principles to the supremacy of G-d, which gives later appeals to Christian values a constitutional ring, even though the text names no religion.
  • Discretion without criteria. Section 33 lets the Prime Minister choose who exercises presidential power, so the choice reflects whatever the government and its consultation prefer.
  • Protection for individuals, not institutions. Section 19 shields a person’s conscience, mainly in schools and oaths, but it does not reach state ceremonies, civil-service prayers, precedence or clerical roles.
  • Custom and ceremony. Parliament opens with prayers led by Anglican clergy, and the table of precedence lists the Chairman of the Barbados Christian Council among the nation’s highest officials, above the Deputy President of the Senate and the heads of the Defence Force and the Police. There is no equivalent place for other faiths.
  • Appointed, not elected. Seven Independent Senators are chosen by the President alone, with no published criteria, and one of them is an Anglican priest.
  • Old laws that wait. The blasphemous libel law, a colonial survival, gives the state a tool to defend one faith’s sensibilities, as set out in the Laws section.
What Supporters Would Say

Supporters would say that Barbados has no state church, that section 19 protects conscience, that the Acting President was properly appointed after consultation with the Opposition and holds only a ceremonial office, that clergy have long served the public in many roles, that most Barbadians are Christian, and that a reference to G-d in a preamble is common across the Commonwealth. The Nasi replies that majority comfort and legal form are exactly how structural privilege hides in plain sight, and that a minority that cannot count on the majority’s good will needs rules, not habits.

What the Nasi Asks For
  • Replace the preamble’s reference to the supremacy of G-d with wording that does not rest the nation’s founding on one belief.
  • Set clear, neutral criteria for designating an Acting President, with rotation, published reasons and consultation beyond the two main parties.
  • End prayers or religious observance as part of civil, parliamentary and public-service functions, or make them genuinely plural and optional.
  • Extend the protection in section 19 to state ceremonies and public employment, and give other faith communities a place in the table of precedence.
  • Publish criteria and a nomination process for the seven Independent Senate seats, with either plural religious representation or none, and require clergy to declare church interests.
  • Repeal the blasphemous libel law.

Political Biography of the Nasi

Kasa Baruh, Dama di Louzada, Nasi of the Dutch-Brazilian Nasión from Barbados, writes as a refugee. This political biography records, in the Nasi’s own account, the events that brought the Dreadlist into being. Matters bearing on the Democratic Labour Party are set out under “On Familiarity with the Democratic Labour Party.”

As of November 2026, the Nasi’s legal process in the Netherlands is ongoing, which makes the operation of this page, and the Nasi’s activity, high-risk. Nonetheless, in the Nasi’s eyes, it is honest work.

A Political Family

The Nasi comes from a political family. Lance Neville Trehane King I, the father of Lance Neville King II, and the cousins of Ione Springer were a political people.

Lance Neville Trehane King I and Sir Errol Walton Barrow both served in the Second World War, King at sea and Barrow in the air.

Sir Hugh Springer, Sir Errol Walton Barrow and many other Springers and Barrows took part in active party politics, most especially in the Democratic Labour Party, which for one reason or another was popular with the Eurafrican Jewish community.

Lance Neville King II inherited the remnants of this culture. Given his treatment, however, his children were burnt out and disappointed by the party, by its neglect and by its poor treatment of its stalwarts, and shifted to the Barbados Labour Party.

The Nasi stood as the sole exception.

The Roots of a Political Life

In 2023 the Nasi came to hold dearly a queer lover, and it was through that attachment that the Nasi became politically inclined. The service of the Nasi’s grandfather, Neville King (Lance Neville King II), further moved the Nasi to choose the Democratic Labour Party in particular.

The Nasi’s grandfather was awarded for forty years of service by Freundel Stuart during his administration.

1 · EQUALS and the BYDC

The Nasi gained entry to the BYDC through the Young Democrats, where the Nasi spoke positively of Tyra Trotman’s efforts. The Nasi became an informal liaison between the BYDC and EQUALS, a role that became more central after the Nasi’s formal exit from the Democratic Labour Party.

Following the resignation from the Democratic Labour Party, the Nasi worked closely with EQUALS and the BYDC, and for a brief period served as Editorial Lead. Disputes over the publication of EQUALS’ achievements permanently soured the Nasi’s relationship with its leadership.

Caleb Braithwaite, in particular, threatened legal action against the Nasi for speaking too boldly at his expense. The matter concerned the unpaid labour of youths working in the BYDC and the overworking of Kemara Harris.

Having formally severed ties with the BYDC, the Nasi resolved to remain within the sphere of EQUALS, and confided to many the Nasi’s concerns about what the Democratic Labour Party had become. The campaign of 2025 against Ralph Thorne and the Democratic Labour Party then incurred the displeasure of the Barbados Labour Party and, by extension, EQUALS. From that point open and covert hostility and queer rivalries followed.

2 · The Trim Dispute and the Poetic Feud

It was articulated to the Nasi that the Nasi’s form of advocacy could bring danger to the queer community of Barbados. The Nasi was denied a platform on the League of Young Socialists podcast, with Roshanna Trim citing “public backlash,” and the Dama stated that the Dama would remember Trim’s refusal.

Roshanna Trim confided in the matter with her brother, Rondell Trim, who subsequently issued threats on her behalf, stating that the Dama could, or would, be blacklisted from Government employment for her proclivities.

The Dama felt bullied and chose, though briefly, to conform with the assimilationist policies of EQUALS and its leadership. This did not last, and the Dama eventually professed that Rondell and Roshanna Trim would one day be forced to “kiss the ring of the Lousada.”

The ensuing conflict became well known in some circles, and many queer people took sides, usually at the Dama’s expense.

The Dama accused other queer poets of covertly taking the Dama’s fashion-photo-shoot poetry, and was distressed that those poets received more likes and support on Instagram within the queer community.

The poetic feud persists to this day.

3 · Refuge in the Jewish Community of Barbados

These pressures led the Nasi to seek refuge in the Jewish Community of Barbados. The Nasi was appalled by that community’s management of the Nasi’s heritage, and withdrew into insularity for at least a year, rarely leaving the house and keeping almost exclusively to a very large family across Bridgetown.

This too came to an end. The Nasi’s politics, Jewish identity and Sephardic linguistic traits caused the family great concern for its reputation, though a number of the young Lousadas were inspired to reclaim Spanish.

4 · The Family and the Reckoning

The Barrow-Montefiore were the least pleased of all. Several found the Nasi’s political inclinations unbearable to their daily lives, and although the Nasi’s proclamation of Jewishness was already known, not every member was willing to engage with it.

Conflicts with the Barbados Labour Party, EQUALS, the Jewish Community, the Democratic Labour Party, conspiracy theorists and queerphobes strained these relations further. The Nasi’s mother issued a death threat, a proclamation of honour or religious murder, in order to ensure that the Nasi remained the boy she had foreseen achieving great deeds. One of the Nasi’s brothers did not threaten death, but expressed the same sentiment as the mother, and abandoned the Nasi as well.

5 · The Walls Close In

With the walls closing in, the Dama sought escape before the inevitable. Many individuals sought the Dama’s demise, and some affiliated with the Democratic Labour Party openly declared that the Dama would be murdered.

The Dama staged an impromptu protest against the President of Barbados, Jeffrey Bostic, outside the St. Ann’s base. There the Dama was issued a death threat, should the Dama record the conduct of a particular officer on public property. Cadets afterwards addressed the Dama with a slur, which demoralised the Dama.

The conduct of the President, the husband of Karina Greenidge, and of others within the party, marked by xenophobia, overzealousness and bigotry, threw the Dama into disrepute and then into social exile.

It seems that the Friends of Democracy wanted the Dama’s skills but not the mandate of protecting the vulnerable. Whereas other queer people had support networks, in EQUALS, in friends or in a community, the Dama stood alone in a political war that the Dama was badly losing.

The Nasi campaigned for the recognition of refugees and for their protection within Barbados. The Nasi published several media campaigns on the history of the Nasi’s community and on Spinozism. The Nasi threatened the Government with a self-destructive protest unless it ended the “cultural genocide of her people,” and openly wrote treatises against figures such as Ralph Thorne and institutions such as the Barbados Jewish Community.

8 · The Question of Israel

The Nasi was vocal in support of the Israeli people, openly breaking the norms of Barbadian culture and media, as a Jewish minority, to speak against the erosion of the rights of dual nationals and Israelis within or on the island.

Caribbean Against Apartheid in Palestine (CAAP) pursued political objectives that reached beyond the courtroom: that Barbados recognise the State of Palestine and cease to validate Israel diplomatically. Through a motion before the High Court, brought by the Caribbean Movement for Peace and Integration and led by CAAP’s secretary, it sought declarations that Israel is committing genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity and operates an apartheid regime; a declaration that the Director of Public Prosecutions’ failure to act breached the Genocide Act and Article 11 of the Constitution; strict screening of Israeli passport holders at every port of entry; and the prosecution or deportation of persons involved, regardless of diplomatic status.

A note on the wording. In the Nasi’s analysis, the motion effectively criminalised Israeli presence in Barbados. Israelis are conscripted into military service, so a motion that sought the prosecution or deportation of anyone “involved,” in language left vague, could reach any Israeli who has served. What “Gay Agenda” Has Meant

For decades, opponents of LGBTQ rights have used the phrase “gay agenda” as if it were a secret plan to corrupt society. It is a conspiracy-style label: it takes ordinary requests, such as safety at school, equal treatment at work and the freedom to love openly, and makes them sound sinister.

In Barbados you can hear the same move in talk of “LGBTQ ideology” being imported from Europe.

What the Manifesto Actually Says

Read plainly, its core is simple: human rights must apply equally to everyone, whatever their sexuality, gender identity, religion or ancestry, and tradition or majority opinion is not a good enough reason to deny anyone their dignity.

It adds three further ideas: queer people should have a voice inside religious life; institutions that hold a community’s property should answer to that community; and groups wronged in the past should be able to seek recognition and repair, with evidence and due process.

Why Reclaiming the Narrative Matters

Whoever tells the story shapes the law. When a minority is described only as a threat, laws like blasphemous libel, vague online-speech offences and gang-style association offences become easier to use against it. When that minority tells its own story, as neighbours, believers, workers and heirs to the island’s history, those laws become harder to defend.

Reclaiming a smear has a long history. “Queer” began as an insult and is now a word many people use with pride. Taking “gay agenda” and answering it with a plain list of equal-rights principles drains the label of its fear, and it tells young queer people that they do not have to accept the villain’s role written for them.

Where People Will Disagree

Not everyone will accept every part. The manifesto uses sharp language about religion and states, including Christian nationalism and Israel; it proposes reparations and “laws of return” whose fairness and workability are debated; and it is tied closely to the author’s own identity and ancestry.

You can agree with equal dignity under the law and still question some of these proposals. The manifesto itself says remedies must be subject to evidence, due process and the rights of others.

Structural Powerism

Structural Powerism is the ability for an institution, in a sociological, legal or communal sense to enforce actions of dominance, in defiance of ethical, egalitarian, or democratic norms to ensure a result contrary to the interests of an asymmetrically weaker party.

It is not that the individual victim to structural powerism has a fundamental lesser value or argument toward their interests, but that the victim is structurally disadvantaged by the power dynamic of a literally, or asymmetrically superior party. My rebuttal & articulation toward: Senator Ryan Walters

First, we must give credit where credit is due; Senator Ryan Walters has brought attention to a pertinent fact, and that is that this economy is overdependent on tourism. It is true that although the economy is doing well, we have yet to see the benefits of trickle-down economics — which perhaps is why the Government is now taking an expressed interest in facilitating investments from Barbadians, into perhaps, local Barbadian entities.

However, I sincerely believe the Senator’s perspective toward the influence the Gobernador holds over the national investment policy of the Ministry of Finance is perhaps overstated. El Gobernador is a technocrat, a man with a doctorate and expertise in economics, who deals in promoting financial stability, primarily.

Thus, we must understand the role of el Gobernador to be that of a protector and a guiding hand, rather than the force that decides how the Government will choose to invest its taxes and direct its financial operations. This primarily falls upon the Ministry of Finance and all other pertinent parties secondarily.

Bim-Pay & The Technocracy

El Gobernador has introduced technological advancement and encouraged the progression of Barbados’ technological integration within global systems, and further, the integration of a nationwide digital finance and payment system.

The public seems lost as to the implications and value of an apparatus that provides tremendous ease in transferring funds from peer to peer — given the limitations of local banking systems and their cross integration. Additionally, it advances financial inclusion, providing access to a digital financial institution operated by the Government — for all Barbadians, even if they lack access to another private financial institution, which I might mention, are not immune to their own share of economic collapses and scandals.

I will provide examples:

In1986, Trade Confirmers, a financial institution that was unregulated by the Central Bank, collapsed. It offered high interest rates, which did not serve to protect the stakeholders of this institution in any manner. When it collapsed, depositors lost funds — and this hurt families.

The circumstance was of such note that it triggered legislative change, which forced the Central Bank’s hand; now, today, all such similar institutions come under the regulation of the Central Bank.

In2005, Olympus Bank and Trust, a financial institution that was owned by a Canadian hedge fund, was forced to close its doors by the Central Bank due to the disappearance of over 130$ Million. The Bank was being investigated by the Securities Exchange Commission for a breach of legislative policy.

According to court filings by the receiver, RSM Richter, the missing money appears to have moved through several Caribbean-based entities, with its present location and the amount recoverable unclear; the action against Olympus was taken over the bank’s failure to comply with the provisions of the International Financial Services Act.

Source: Source: Family Wealth Report, “Barbados Investigates Two More Banks.”

This is not to draw doubt, nor discourage trust in our financial institutions, which are foreign or privately owned; this is to draw attention toward the protection a platform such as Bimpay provides the average consumer against such things should they occur. Consider it, if nothing else, a contingency — which should be your first choice, like an immunization.

It provides the people of Barbados not just a digital wallet, but financial security and protection in case privately owned banks fail. But, in terms of the micro impact of this policy, think of this: you seek to purchase an item at present, it is 5000$. In the rising trend of crime across the Caribbean and Barbados, carrying such a sum may be more of a risk than it is worth. Bim-pay makes this risk a non-factor. The funds are digital and instantly transferable from peer to peer, like a regular transaction — but more secure.

Even for the Government — if you had remembered that time where all Barbadians were given around 300 BD$ before the prior election, lines were stretching far and wide, all to collect their sum em pessoa. Now the government will be able to distribute such funds — instantly. Bimpay is an integral, vital, technological innovation that provides financial empowerment — and it does not harm our citizens.

Some critics of Bimpay, I have seen, make arguments about “The Jews” controlling the nation, or something to that effect. It is foolish, it is outrageous, it is disrespect and it warrants a malicious communications charge.

Yet, the Gobernador is an honorable man who accepts criticism, but cares little for what others may say onto himself at a personal level, unlike Minister Wilfred Abrahams, who has proven to pursue those in bad faith aggressively. Both men are of great esteem, but only one of them has the patience to tolerate foolishness.

El Gobernador is a methodical and strategic man. I envy his fortitude, his stoicism, his ability to remain composed, calm, and focused on his mission despite bigoted statements, or those like Senator Ryan Walters who unfairly attribute blame to El Gobernador.

The Accusation of “Contradiction” by Senator Ryan Walters

Senator Ryan Walters has claimed that, by seeking ‘backup’ mechanisms and loans in case of instances that may provoke sudden economic decline or collapse, El Gobernador contradicts his proclaimed stance that the economy is growing and stable.

But I understand this criticism from the Senator to be indicative of the type of Leader he is. Where-as el Gobernador seeks contingencies, backup plans, and endeavors to ensure that his people are not left without should they be in need, Senator Ryan Walters portrays himself as a man who values rhetorical strength, over the practical protection and security of the people of his nation.

Certainly, it would be remiss of anyone to fault any individual within this nation for keeping savings for a Rainy Day. Yet, when we look at our Gobernador, who seeks to do the same, in tandem with our Prime Minister, we find men who point and question the nature of these protections against the aforementioned ‘rainy day’.
